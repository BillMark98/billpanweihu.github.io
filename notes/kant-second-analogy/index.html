Why Kant needs causality before he can have events

The Second Analogy argues that objective succession is impossible without an a priori principle of causality. A note on what the argument does and does not establish.

Every perception I have is successive. I take in one thing after another, always. So how do I ever distinguish my looking around a thing from something happening to it?

This is the problem the Second Analogy of Experience is built to solve, and stating it that way already does most of the work.

The asymmetry Kant is pointing at

Walk around a house. Your perceptions arrive in order: roof, then door, then garden. Now watch a ship drift downstream. Your perceptions also arrive in order: upstream position, then downstream position.

Both sequences are successive as experiences. But only one of them is a sequence in the object.

With the house, the order was up to you. You could have started at the garden. With the ship, you could not have perceived the downstream position first — not because it would have been inconvenient, but because there was nothing there to perceive.

So there is a difference between the two cases, and it cannot be found by inspecting the succession of perceptions, because the succession of perceptions is the same in both. Kant's question is what the difference consists in.

Necessity, and where it has to come from

His answer: in the second case the order of my perceptions is necessary — it could not have been otherwise. In the first case it is arbitrary.

And necessity is not something you can read off experience. Experience shows you what did happen, never what had to. Hume was right about that, and Kant does not dispute it.

If the order of appearances in an event is necessary, and necessity cannot be extracted from what is given, then the necessity has to be contributed. It must be an a priori rule that the understanding brings to the appearances, not one it finds among them.

That rule is causality: every event is determined by a preceding state according to a rule.

The distinction that makes the argument work

Here is the part that is easy to lose, and losing it makes the argument look absurdly strong.

Kant is not establishing any particular causal law. He is not deducing that heat expands metals, or that the sun warms the stone. Those are empirical discoveries, found the ordinary way, and any of them could turn out to be wrong.

What he is establishing is the general principle that objective succession is lawfully determined at all — that if something genuinely happened, there is some rule under which it follows from what preceded it, whether or not anyone ever finds that rule.

The general principle is a condition for the possibility of experience. The particular laws are contents of experience. Collapse the two and Kant is claiming to derive physics from armchair reflection; keep them apart and he is claiming something much narrower and much more defensible.

In the Second Analogy, Kant argues that objective succession in time — that something happens — cannot be grounded in the mere succession of perceptions, since apprehension is always successive. For succession to belong to the object rather than to the subject, the order of appearances must be necessary and rule-governed. This necessity is expressed in the a priori principle of causality. The Second Analogy therefore establishes not any particular empirical causal law, but the general principle that lawful causal determination is a necessary condition for the possibility of experience itself.

A note on the categories of quantity

A separate observation, from the Axioms of Intuition rather than the Analogies, that I find clarifying about how the categories are supposed to operate at all.

The three categories of quantity are unity, plurality, totality. Read as a list of kinds of quantity, they are unremarkable. Read as a sequence, they describe how apprehending a magnitude actually goes:

I first take the object in as one. Then I run through its parts. Then I grasp it again as a whole — but now as a whole composed of those parts, which is not what I had at the start.

There is a Chinese formulation of stages of understanding that has the same shape — 先是大致印象，然后细节，最后在这些基础上重新统摄: first the rough impression, then the details, then a re-integration on the basis of both. The third stage returns to the first in form while differing from it entirely in content.

Whether or not Kant intended the triads to be read developmentally, reading them that way makes the table of categories look less like a taxonomy and more like a description of an activity.
