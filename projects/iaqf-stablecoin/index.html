This is team work. Submitted for the IAQF 2026 Academic Competition by the Bootstrapper-IAQF team. The write-up below describes the project as a whole, not solely my contribution. Please credit the full team for any use of it.

The event

On 10 March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank failed. Circle held roughly $3.3bn of USDC reserves there. Over the following weekend USDC — a coin whose entire purpose is to be worth one dollar — traded as low as $0.87.

What makes this interesting is not that the peg broke but how unevenly it broke. The same asset traded at materially different prices across venues at the same moment, and the dislocation persisted far longer than a functioning arbitrage would allow. The question the project asks is: what does liquidity actually do during a stablecoin run, and can you see the stress coming?

Data

CoinAPI tick-level flat files across multiple venues and stablecoin pairs, resampled to a one-minute master panel. A separate download/ package handles ingestion through CoinAPI and CCXT.

The ETL is the unglamorous majority of the work. Tick data from multiple exchanges disagrees about timestamps, symbol conventions, and what constitutes a trade; reconciling that into a panel where a cross-venue spread means something is most of the engineering.

The CoinAPI flat files are licensed data and are not redistributable. The repository ships the pipeline and the processed outputs, not the raw ticks.

CMLSI — a cross-market liquidity stress index

Individual liquidity measures each capture part of stress and none of it cleanly: quoted spread, depth at top of book, realised volatility, cross-venue basis, trade imbalance. They are also highly correlated, so stacking them into a regression produces unstable coefficients.

The approach is to take the first principal component across the standardised measures as a single stress factor. The resulting index — CMLSI — gives one interpretable series per venue that can be used as both a dependent and an explanatory variable in what follows.

Dynamics

With the index in hand, the modelling is conventional time-series work, chosen because it is interpretable rather than because it is novel:

  • VAR / VARX across venue stress series, with macro and crypto-specific exogenous variables
  • Impulse response functions — when stress spikes on one venue, how does it propagate, and with what lag?
  • FEVD — how much of each venue's stress variance is attributable to the others?
  • HMM regime detection, to identify the transition into and out of the crisis regime without hand-picking dates
  • An autoencoder-augmented VAR, where a learned low-dimensional encoding of the panel supplements the linear state

The last two are the extensions where the model earns or loses its keep, and the repository contains the error analysis to judge that.

The counterfactual

The final stage simulates the microstructure under the reserve-composition and disclosure requirements of the GENIUS Act. The counterfactual is necessarily a model, not evidence — but it makes the policy argument concrete by stating explicitly what would have had to be different, and by how much, for the depeg to have been contained.

Repository

A proper package (iaqf/) rather than a pile of notebooks: a config registry, ETL, EDA, feature construction and PCA, the model layer, diagnostics, the simulation, and a CLI entry point:

python -m iaqf.main --stages features var diagnostics simulation

Plus five test modules, seven notebooks, and two LaTeX papers in docs/. Fourteen commits with messages that track the actual research arc — restructuring, VAR error analysis, the HMM and autoencoder extensions.
